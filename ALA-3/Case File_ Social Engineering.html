Social Engineering Attacks & Major Security Breaches

The biggest breaches rarely start with code. They start with a convincing lie.

01

The Attack Lifecycle

🔍
STEP 1

Research

Scout the target's name, role, and habits.

☎
STEP 2

Contact

Reach out by email, phone, or in person.

🤝
STEP 3

Build Trust

Impersonate someone familiar or in charge.

🔓
STEP 4

Exploit

Get a click, a password, or a plugged-in drive.

🧹
STEP 5

Cover Tracks

Delete logs, keep access quiet.

02

Three Ways In

EXHIBIT A

✉ Phishing

A fake message impersonates a trusted sender to harvest credentials or spread malware.

Email lure → Fake login page → Credentials stolen
EXHIBIT B

☎ Pretexting

An attacker invents a believable scenario — "IT support," "a vendor" — to extract information.

Fabricated role → Earned trust → Data handed over
EXHIBIT C

💾 Baiting

A tempting physical or digital item — a USB drive, a free download — hides malware inside.

Tempting item → Curiosity → Malware runs
03

When It Worked: Real Breaches

40M
CARD NUMBERS STOLEN
PHISHING · TARGET, 2013

Vendor credentials, nationwide checkout malware

Attackers phished Target's HVAC vendor for network credentials, then installed malware on checkout systems nationwide.

Could've been stopped: Isolate vendor access from payment networks.
130
ACCOUNTS HIJACKED
PRETEXTING · TWITTER, 2020

Fake IT support, verified accounts seized

Callers posing as IT support tricked employees into a fake VPN login page, harvesting credentials used to seize verified accounts.

Could've been stopped: Verify IT requests through a separate, known channel.
14
MONTHS TO ERADICATE
BAITING · BUCKSHOT YANKEE, 2008

A parking-lot USB drive, a classified network

A malware-laden USB drive left in a parking lot was plugged into a military laptop, letting a worm spread across classified networks.

Could've been stopped: Ban unknown removable media; disable autorun.
04

Building Human Firewalls

Technology stops some attacks. Habits stop the rest.

Verify independently

Call back on a number you already know — never one given by the requester.

Pause before clicking

Check the sender address and link destination before acting.

Limit public details

Less personal info online means fewer believable pretexts.

Segment the network

Keep vendor and guest access away from critical systems.

Train and simulate

Regular phishing drills build instinct, not just awareness.

Report immediately

Fast reporting shrinks the time attackers have to act.
